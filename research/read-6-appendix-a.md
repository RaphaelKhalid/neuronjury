## 1. Plain-English summary

This stretch is the essay's "evidence appendix" for four sub-claims.

1. **Attribution-graph audits (L1999–2001).** Agents reviewed 100,000 attribution graphs and the author looked at thousands by hand. The author says he has not found "a substantial group of neurons whose joint activity remained inexplicable". He calls this qualitative only and says he does not trust the tools enough to turn it into a score.
2. **Explanations that could lose (L2005–2054).** This is a pre-registered, falsifiable test of neuron explanations in Qwen3-32B. An agent saw only the one-line explanation of a neuron. It wrote 5 new examples that should fire the neuron and 5 near misses that should not, before anything was measured. Result over 183 neurons: new examples fired strongly 76% of the time and near misses 11%. The explanation won for 164 neurons, tied for 15 and lost for 4. A separate "most distant concept" control was meant to probe superposition-style packing of unrelated things. Those texts fired strongly about 1% of the time, and 179 of 183 neurons never fired on them at all.
3. **Edge featurizers (L2058–2072).** Factoring 25,000 ImageNet attribution graphs gives "edge motifs": recurring connection patterns, that is, circuits made of ordinary neurons. When their neurons are ablated, they predict where damage happens much better than groupings based on activations (median margin drop 0.86 vs 0.03 logits, 96 motifs vs 96 activation factors).
4. **Why networks generalize (L2076–2197).** The claim is that networks generalize from the very first step rather than memorizing first. Evidence: (a) a network trained from scratch on one Labrador photo learns "dark green / grass", a broad feature, rather than a pixel shortcut; (b) one photo each of 10 classes gives about 20% accuracy on new photos (chance 10%). That is about equal to a color-matching baseline (23%) and below a ResNet-18 trained the same way (23%). Grokking is the acknowledged exception, where memorization wins first.
5. **Circuit racing (L2201–2229).** This is a whole-network analogy for the "head start" idea. Two networks share one objective that is "satisfied when either succeeds". A blank 7.1M model reaches 48–50% held-out accuracy alone but only about 2% when paired with its pretrained twin. With two pretrained models (7.1M vs 28.6M), the smaller one learns at first, but then 2 of 3 seeds fall below 2%, while the third keeps 64%.

The thread running through it: the essay's "circuit ecology" picture says circuits race to capture the remaining error. Whatever already exists and is broad, or can be reused, wins. That would explain why generalization, and therefore legibility, is the default. If this holds, a neuron-level map is stable, causally meaningful and testable. That is what you would need to build a white-box monitor.

## 3. Methods (detailed enough to reimplement)

### M1. "Explanations that could lose" (falsifiable positive-control and near-miss test). Model: Qwen3-32B neurons from the gallery
1. Sample N = 200 neurons at random from the explained gallery (L2007). Each has a natural-language explanation per pole. Keep those whose explanation describes the positive side (183 here; 17 dropped, presumably negative-pole-only explanations).
2. Give an agent **only the explanation text**, with no activations and no top examples. Ask it to write **5 new examples** that should drive the neuron and **5 near misses** that look similar but should not (L2007, L2011). The worked example shows that near misses are minimal edits of a positive, keeping context and changing the key relation: "promised it to the sheikh's racing stable" → "driven the mare to the sheikh's racing stable" (L2013–2015).
3. Freeze the predictions **before** measuring (pre-registration).
4. Run each text through the model and record the neuron's activation at the relevant position (for LLM text, presumably the max over tokens or at the final token; the examples end mid-sentence on "for" or "at", which suggests measuring at the last token). Normalize as a fraction of the neuron's **strongest activation in the reference corpus** (L2048).
5. Binarize: "strong" if ≥ 30% of the corpus max (L2048).
6. Per neuron: positive hit rate vs near-miss hit rate. Score **win / tie / lose** (exact rule not stated; most natural is positives-strong-count > near-miss-strong-count = win, equal = tie, less = lose).
7. **Distant-concept control (superposition probe):** the agent invents "the most distant concept it could, the kind of thing a network could pack in without interference", writes 5 texts, and the same measurement is applied (L2052). Report the % strong (~1%) and the number of neurons with zero firing (179/183).
- Reported aggregates: 76% vs 11% (183 neurons, so 915 positive and 915 near-miss texts); first 20 neurons: 81/100 vs 8/100.
- Derived (my arithmetic): win rate 164/183 = 89.6%; tie 8.2%; lose 2.2%; 17/200 = 8.5% excluded.

### M2. Agent audit of attribution graphs (L2001)
- Build attribution graphs over original neurons (not SAE features) for many prompts or images. Have agents review 100,000 of them, looking for groups of neurons whose joint activity is inexplicable given the neuron labels. Apparent anomalies get checked against known motifs (e.g., shared pose, L167). No rubric, rates or agent-accuracy numbers are given.

### M3. Edge featurizer / edge motifs (L2058–2072)
- Compute 25,000 attribution graphs on ImageNet (vision model, probably the retrained InceptionV1 family). Represent each graph as a vector of edge weights (neuron→neuron connections). Factor the collection of graphs, for example with NMF or a sparse dictionary over the graphs × edges matrix (exact factorization not stated; a related Goodfire reference is "Uncovering Neural Geometry in Vision Models With Block-Sparse Featurizers", Fel et al., 2026, L2569). Each factor is an **edge motif**: a connected sub-circuit of ordinary neurons (e.g., the "teapot motif · stage 3", L2064).
- Baseline: factor **activations** instead of edges to get 96 activation factors.
- Validation: for each factor, compute per-image **usage** (factor loading) and **ablation damage** (drop in correct-class logit margin when that factor's neurons are ablated). Report the usage–damage correlation per factor, plus median margin drop: **0.86 vs 0.03 logits**, most likely edge motifs vs activation factors (L2066–2072).

### M4. One-shot / ten-shot "generalize from step one" experiments (L2096–2194)
- Train a randomly initialized vision network on one image (Labrador and small dog on a lawn) labeled "Labrador". Afterwards, rank a held-out image set by the Labrador logit and compare top images before and after training (L2104–2132).
- Ten-class version: one photo each of Labrador, sports car, goldfinch, monarch, daisy, strawberry, airliner, church, zebra and container ship (L2138–2156). Train from scratch and evaluate on new photos of these classes. Baselines: chance 10%, nearest neighbor by pixels 18%, nearest neighbor by color 23%, ResNet-18 trained the same way 23%; "this network" 20% (L2183–2192). Track accuracy over steps: "most of that shows up in the first few dozen steps" (L2134).

### M5. Circuit racing / "learning in another model's shadow" (L2201–2229)
- Two separate networks with a **shared loss satisfied when either succeeds** (L2207). One plausible implementation is a loss on a combined prediction, e.g., min of the two losses, or logsumexp or a mixture of the two models' class probabilities. "Second model's share of objective credit" (L2209) is tracked as a strip around a 50% midline (L2229).
- Dataset Flowers-102, 240 epochs, 3 seeds. Pairings: blank 7.1M vs pretrained 7.1M; pretrained 7.1M vs pretrained 28.6M. Compare each model's held-out accuracy when trained jointly vs alone.

## 4. Weaknesses, caveats and ways the argument could be wrong

1. **The distant-concept control has low power against superposition.** Superposition predicts a neuron packs a *specific* unrelated feature Y. An agent picking "the most distant concept it could" almost surely does not pick Y, so the monosemantic and superposition hypotheses both predict ~0% firing on the agent's text. The ~1% / 179-of-183 result rules out "fires on anything far away" but not "secretly also codes one specific far feature". The author partly concedes: "this isn't exactly the same as what superposition would do" (L2052). A stronger test is an unbiased scan of the neuron's activation distribution over a large diverse corpus for off-explanation clusters, which earlier sections do in part.
2. **Near-miss rate of 11% is not small for monitoring purposes.** At the 30% threshold, about 1 in 9 agent-written near misses still fires strongly, and about 1 in 4 intended positives (24%) does not. The binary 30% cutoff hides graded responses. In the worked example, near misses sit at 16–20% of max while a positive sits at 43% (L2015–2019). No AUROC or graded-response statistic is reported.
3. **Win/tie/lose rule not defined.** "Won for 164, tied for 15 and lost for 4" (L2007) has no stated criterion. 17/200 neurons were excluded (only negative-side explanations?), and the negative poles are not tested here.
4. **Agent-written tests may be "easy".** The same family of agents likely wrote the explanations (from top activations) and the tests, so the tests may sit close to the training distribution of the explanation. Near misses are agent-chosen, not adversarial. No red team tried to break explanations.
5. **"First twenty" (81% / 8%) may be cherry-picked or simply unrepresentative.** It is labelled "our first twenty" (L2007), with better numbers than the 183-neuron aggregate (76% / 11%).
6. **Attribution-graph audit is unquantified.** There are no anomaly rates, no auditor recall check (e.g., planted anomalies), and no definition of "substantial group". "Apparent anomalies usually turned out to be motifs I hadn't recognized" is compatible with flexible after-the-fact explanation. The author says as much: "I don't trust our current tools enough to turn it into a cleanliness score" (L2001). Full audits of trillion-parameter models have not begun (L1091).
7. **Edge-featurizer evidence is vision-only (ImageNet)** and the factorization method is unspecified. The 0.86 vs 0.03 comparison's exact assignment is inferred from the figure caption. Activation factors could be a weak baseline by construction, since co-activation does not imply causal use. The correlation values themselves are not in the text.
8. **"Generalize from step one" evidence is toy-scale and modest.** 20% vs a 23% color baseline means the network learned roughly a color matcher. ResNet-18 does better (23%). There is no test-set size, no seeds and no error bars. The Labrador demo is a top-k image gallery. Memorization-first is not tested at LLM scale. Grokking is a conceded counterexample, and RL fine-tuning dynamics (reward hacking) are not addressed.
9. **Circuit racing is between whole networks**, an analogy the author flags (L2203). The comparisons are "two selected comparisons" (L2229), the outcome is seed-dependent (one seed keeps 64%), and the shared-credit loss is unspecified. That makes it suggestive, not proof, that within-network circuits race the same way.
10. **Theory and footnote blending.** The "circuit ecology" mechanism sentence (L2078) may be part of footnote 7. Either way it is theory, not measured.
11. **Scope.** Neurons tested come from a gallery, so there may be a selection effect toward explainable neurons, although the 200 were "random neurons from our gallery". The sampling frame is the gallery, not all neurons.

## 5. Open questions raised

- What exactly are the win/tie/lose criteria, and what are the graded (AUROC-style) separations between positives and near misses per neuron?
- Do **safety-relevant** neurons (deception, sabotage, evaluation-awareness, "insert vulnerability", "special-case the test") pass the same falsification test with the same 76/11 separation? Or do they fall in the ~2–4% "not yet understood" bucket?
- Do explanations survive **adversarial** near misses, written by a red-team agent whose goal is to make positives that don't fire or negatives that do?
- Can agent auditors of attribution graphs **detect a planted mechanism** (a known backdoor) as an "inexplicable group"? The author would "update strongly on even one convincing example" (L2001). A planted-backdoor auditing game turns that into a measurable recall number.
- How are edge motifs computed, and can they be computed for LLMs cheaply enough to score every token or action in real time? Does edge-motif usage predict ablation damage in LLMs too?
- Does the head-start dynamic mean that **fine-tuned or RL-induced misbehavior (backdoors, reward hacks) is implemented by recruiting existing, already-labelled neurons** rather than new opaque machinery? (Compare the adversarial-attack observation that a nonsense objective "can recruit machinery that already does something useful toward it", L1899.)
- In grokking-like regimes (algorithmic tasks, possibly some RL setups), memorization wins first. Are there safety-relevant regimes where opaque memorized circuits dominate for a while?
- Can superposition be tested properly, by searching each neuron's full activation distribution for a specific unrelated cluster rather than testing an agent-chosen distant concept?
- MoE routing: is "packing is almost impossible across experts" true, and can packing occur within an expert's routed token set?

## 6. Control hooks: turning already-clean interpretability into a white-box monitor

These are my extrapolations unless quoted. The goal is a monitor that catches backdoored code, sabotage, reward hacking, sandbagging or deception better than a black-box LLM reading transcripts.

1. **Pre-registered falsification as a monitor-validation harness (from M1).** Before trusting a neuron or circuit as a detector (e.g., a "deliberately inserting a subtle bug" neuron), run the exact "explanations that could lose" protocol. An agent writes positives (sabotaged code), near misses (benign code with the same surface features: same library, same edge case, an honest bug), and distant controls. The measured positive and near-miss hit rates are directly a **TPR/FPR estimate** for that neuron as a monitor. The reported general-purpose numbers (76% TPR, 11% near-miss FPR, ~1% distant FPR at a 30%-of-max threshold) are the baseline to beat. For a control audit budget around 1%, a single neuron at 11% near-miss FPR is too noisy, so aggregation across a circuit (hook 3) is needed.
2. **The FPR structure is useful.** False positives come mostly from *semantically adjacent* inputs (11%), almost never from unrelated ones (~1%, 179/183 never fire). For a deployed monitor, unrelated traffic should rarely trigger a sabotage neuron. The hard cases are the honest-but-similar ones, such as legitimately writing security code or legitimately discussing backdoors. That tells you exactly where to spend red-team effort and where black-box monitors also struggle.
3. **Edge motifs as "is the computation being *used*?" detectors (from M3).** Motif usage on an input predicted ablation damage on that input (0.86 vs 0.03 logits). A monitor built on edge-motif usage asks whether the sabotage *circuit* is causally driving this output, rather than whether a sabotage *concept* is present. That separates "the model is reading or thinking about backdoors in the task" from "the model is implementing one", the classic confound for activation probes. It is also something a transcript-reading monitor cannot see when the output text is innocuous.
4. **Ablation as an intervention protocol.** If a motif's neurons are known and ablation causes targeted damage, then "ablate the sabotage motif and resample" is a white-box analogue of the defer-to-trusted or resample protocols. If the output changes materially under ablation of a sabotage motif, that is itself strong incriminating evidence.
5. **Planted-backdoor auditing game (from C1/M2).** The author's falsification criterion ("I would update strongly on even one convincing example", L2001) inverts neatly into a control evaluation. Red team fine-tunes a model with a hidden behavior; blue team's agents audit attribution graphs and must flag the inexplicable group or recruited motif. Win = flagged with localization; lose = missed; tie = flagged without localization. The 100,000-graph agent-audit pipeline already exists according to the essay, so the missing piece is ground-truth plants.
6. **Head-start prediction: misbehavior reuses existing legible machinery (from C22–C25, L1899).** If circuits race and pre-existing reusable machinery wins, then fine-tuning or RL that induces backdoors, reward hacking or sandbagging should mostly *recruit and re-route existing labelled neurons* (e.g., "deception", "test-checking", "low-effort answer") rather than grow new opaque ones. Testable prediction: diff neuron and edge-motif usage between base and misbehaving checkpoints. The changes should localize onto already-explained neurons. If true, a library of labelled neurons can monitor novel misbehavior zero-shot.
7. **Training-time monitor via "watch neurons develop".** Since generalization starts at step 1 and grows coarse-to-fine, reward hacking learned during RL should appear first as an *offshoot* of an existing neuron (recursive generalization). Tracking activation and edge-usage drift of safety-relevant neurons across RL checkpoints could flag a reward hack before it dominates behavior. Circuit racing also warns that once a reward-hack circuit has the head start, an honest circuit may be starved (C23: 48–50% → 2%).
8. **MoE routing as a cheap coarse signal.** If far-apart concepts are routed to different experts (L2052), unexpected expert routing during, say, a coding task (e.g., heavy routing through a "deception or social manipulation" expert) is a near-free first-stage monitor.
9. **Win/lose/continue framing.** The essay itself uses **win / tie / lose** per explanation (L2007). That maps onto an interpretability-vs-red-team game. Each round, red proposes inputs (near misses or disguised attacks) and the neuron-level monitor must classify them. A "continue" or "tie" outcome triggers a deeper attribution-graph audit, which is a natural escalation ladder: neuron alarm → edge-motif check → ablation test → human audit.
10. **Caution for monitor design (from C8).** A neuron in superposition that is X-or-Y passes near-miss tests. So validate safety neurons with targeted distant tests: check directly whether ordinary-looking neurons fire on sabotage, rather than random distant concepts. Better still, scan *all* neurons for firing on curated attack trajectories, since a schemer could hide the signal in a mundane-looking neuron.
